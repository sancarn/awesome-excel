# Curation guidelines

Awesome Excel celebrates resources that make Excel more useful, enjoyable or surprising. We want each entry to give readers a clear reason to explore it.

When suggesting a resource, start with this question: **What makes it exceptional, and why would an Excel user choose it?**

## What belongs here

- **Add-ins** that meaningfully improve everyday Excel workflows or extend its capabilities, whether built with VSTO, COM, OfficeJS or VBA.
- **Templates** that solve a real problem particularly well: workbooks you use regularly or would recommend without hesitation.
- **Exceptional examples and art** that make you think, "How did they make this in Excel?!" This could be inventive models, visualisations, simulations, games or artwork. Outstanding execution can also earn a place when the techniques are familiar.
- **Formulas and data pipelines** that offer an especially useful or elegant approach to working in Excel, including reusable functions and Power Query workflows.
- **Learning resources** that stand out for their clarity, depth, originality or practical value.
- **External tools** that meaningfully improve working with Excel files or moving data into and out of Excel.
- **Development resources** that offer substantial value for building Excel integrations, functions and add-ins, or working with workbook files.

Tools primarily aimed at VBA developers belong in [awesome-vba](https://github.com/sancarn/awesome-vba). Using VBA does not itself disqualify a resource here: a VBA-powered workbook or add-in can still be valuable to Excel users. For showcases, consider how Excel's spreadsheet or visual features contribute to the achievement.

Each list has its own quality threshold. Falling below Awesome Excel's threshold does not establish a case for inclusion in Awesome VBA. Accessible VBA source alone is insufficient; a recommendation there should identify specific value for its audience, such as exceptional programming, reusable engineering, an instructive implementation or an outstanding finished experience.

## What makes something awesome

### A clear purpose and audience

Assess a resource against what it promises and who it serves. A utility should demonstrate a meaningful practical benefit. A teaching resource should help its audience understand or apply something particularly well. Art and showcases can qualify through creativity or craftsmanship without being practically useful.

A utility with weak practical value needs an independent case for inclusion as a showcase. Recognising the work behind it does not establish that case.

### A reason to choose it

Explain what stands out beyond a feature list. Where native Excel features or other resources already address the task, identify the advantage: substantially better usability, automation, reliability, explanation or capabilities. An excellent implementation of a familiar workflow can qualify.

For example, importing data into a table or providing a checklist may be useful, but the submission should explain what makes that particular resource exceptional. A resource can complement existing entries without replacing them or outperforming them at their own specialties.

### Quality of the result

Judge the resulting experience, insight or capability. Hundreds of worksheets, large amounts of code, months of work or a manual recreation of a familiar game demonstrate dedication; they do not independently establish exceptional quality.

For showcases, explain what stands out beyond the surprise that something runs in Excel. This could be inventive spreadsheet techniques, exceptional artwork, thoughtful interaction, substantial educational value or outstanding execution.

Straightforward techniques can produce exceptional work. Technical mystery, complexity and formula-only implementations are not requirements; assess the quality and inventiveness of their use.

### Understanding that carries over

Learning resources should demonstrate how they teach particularly well. Clear explanations, worked examples, meaningful practice and interactive exploration can all contribute.

A consistently excellent Excel blog can earn a recommendation through its body of work. An individual post should be exceptional in its own right or have an exceptional reason to stand alone in the list. Choose the level of recommendation that best represents the value: the blog as a whole or the particular post.

Judge visualisations by the understanding they enable for their intended audience. Nesting highlights, syntax references and structural breakdowns may expose a formula's structure without explaining what its parts produce, why they are used or how they combine. More detail or interaction is valuable when it makes ideas easier to understand or apply.

### Enough evidence to recommend it

Provide accessible material that supports the inclusion case: a working tool, workbook, substantive lessons, source or a convincing demonstration. Free downloads and accessible code make assessment easier; they do not by themselves justify inclusion.

Review claims in proportion to their importance. Correctness is central to a formula converter; clear data provenance matters to a data service. Unresolved problems with a resource's main promise can justify deferring or declining it.

Be clear about what was checked. A creator's screenshots, documentation or compatibility claims are different evidence from running a workbook or verifying an add-in in Excel. Static inspection can support a review, but should not be presented as gameplay, runtime or compatibility testing. Historical examples can still qualify; describe relevant version requirements.

### Room for improvement

Resources need not be perfect or feature-complete. Assess whether limitations undermine the reason for recommending them. Feature requests, rough edges and ordinary licensing traffic are not automatic grounds for rejection. Consider security and privacy findings on their evidence and impact.

Keep the merit decision separate from improvement feedback. Fixing access, compatibility or implementation problems may allow reconsideration, but does not automatically make a resource awesome.

## Suggesting a resource

Open an issue or submit a pull request with:

- A direct link to the resource or its original creator. Prefer the relevant workbook, release or tool over a general promotional page, and omit referral parameters.
- A short, factual description of what it does and a specific explanation of what makes it worth including.
- Its intended audience and, where useful, why someone would choose it over relevant alternatives.
- Enough material to assess it, with any limits on what you have personally verified.
- Significant costs, access restrictions, compatibility requirements and dependencies, such as macros, an account, external services or a particular Excel version.

Commercial resources are welcome. Use the [README's symbols](README.md#symbology) to identify paid content and password-protected workbooks where applicable. Keep listing descriptions concise, accurate and supported by the resource; avoid universal claims such as "works with any table" unless they can be substantiated.
